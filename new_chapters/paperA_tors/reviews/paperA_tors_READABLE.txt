Elicitation Results Are Instrument-Relative: A Certified Interview-Compatible Recommender for Measuring Cold-Start Preference Elicitation ALEKSANDRA MAKAROVA, [Affiliation to be completed], United Kingdom A cold-start recommender asks a new user a few questions and then ranks a catalogue, and the literature on which questions to ask evaluates each selection strategy through whatever recommender consumes the answers. We show that the published ranking of the classical cold-start selection heuristics is not a property of the heuristics. We hold the questions, users, answers, budget and candidate pool fixed, and vary only the recommender that reads the answers. The order reported by Rashid et al. reproduces under our reconstruction of the node model of Golbandi et al., and inverts under a frontier-accuracy consumer, on full and tail NDCG@10. Every gap that decides one of the three distinct orderings is separated by a paired per-user bootstrap over 10,000 held-out users. An elicitation result is therefore a claim about a strategy and the recommender it was measured through. Measuring strategies in isolation requires a recommender that can be interviewed: one that folds an arbitrary-length, mixedchannel set of answers into its user representation without per-user retraining and without giving up full-profile accuracy. We state four requirements such an instrument must meet: frontier-parity accuracy, an open set-valued input, monotonicity, and answers that carry sign and intensity. Under one shared ML-25M protocol, the published literature is bifurcated between an accuracy corner and an elicitation corner, and no system meets all four. We then build one that does: a frozen, certified recommender with a permutationinvariant set encoder over its latent space, into which items, out-of-catalogue concepts and free-text phrases enter as a direction paired with a value. The instrument reaches full/tail NDCG@10 of 0.3496/0.2491 against a RecVAE bar of 0.3540/0.2497, from implementations that reproduce the published ML-20M numbers to the third decimal; it is statistically indistinguishable from the bar on the tail, 1.2% short on the full profile, and passes a verification battery with one item per requirement and two integrity controls. With the instrument fixed, answerability becomes a measured quantity: a held-out user has rated 25.2% of the hundred most popular films but can answer 53.0% of 1,031 concept questions. The item and concept channels can then be compared under one protocol, and the comparison is criterion-relative: ranked by discrimination, the concept channel wins on both metrics at every budget because a maximally divisive film is one almost nobody has seen, while under answerability-weighted rankings the item channel wins full accuracy. The confound is not hypothetical, and it changes published conclusions. The classical cold-start selection heuristics were validated on the recommenders of their day. Rashid et al. [45] report the order helf > entRopy0 > populaRity (helf is the harmonic mean of an item’s rating entropy and its log frequency; entRopy0 is rating entropy with “not rated” counted as an outcome; populaRity asks about the most-rated items), and that order has been carried forward as a property of the heuristics. We ran all three under one fixed interview protocol, identical users, an identical budget of eight questions, an identical answer model, a question bank fixed in advance and one candidate pool, and varied only the recommender that consumes the answers. Under our reconstruction of the node model of Golbandi et al. [18], a neighbourhood-style consumer closest in kind to the user-based models the heuristics were validated on, the published order reproduces. Under a frontier-accuracy consumer it inverts, with entRopy0 leading on both full and tail NDCG@10. Under the strongest full-profile model under our protocol, restricted to the likes among the asked items because a binary indicator input can represent nothing else, populaRity leads on the full profile. Six consumers realise three distinct orderings, and every gap that decides one of those three is separated by a paired per-user bootstrap over 10,000 held-out users (Section 7.1). A paper reporting any one of these orderings in isolation, with its own recommender, would be reporting something true and non-transferable. This is the concrete sense in which elicitation results are instrument-relative, and it is why this article is about the instrument rather than about a policy. Before one can study how to interview a cold-start user, one needs a recommender that can be interviewed: a model that folds an arbitrary-length, mixed-channel set of answers into its user representation and re-ranks a large catalogue, without per-user retraining and without trading away full-profile accuracy. We argue that such a recommender is a measuring instrument for the elicitation-policy literature, that its requirements are more demanding than any single published system we surveyed meets, and that the requirements are not desiderata but conditions a recommender must satisfy before a strategy comparison measured through it means anything. Accuracy at full profile does not imply accuracy after an interview. One number makes the point concretely, and it concerns a baseline rather than our instrument. The strongest full-profile model under our protocol, RecVAE [47], when restricted to the likes among the asked items, scores below our instrument by 0.029 full NDCG@10 after eight questions, each system on its own best question-selection strategy, on 10,000 held-out users with a paired confidence interval excluding zero. The same model is above ours at full profile. Whatever else separates the two systems, a strong full-profile score is not by itself sufficient for a strong short interview. What this article measures, and what it does not. The answers folded here come from recorded behaviour: an item answer is a user’s own rating, a concept answer a log watch-lift over raw counts. That is a deliberate scope. It lets us measure exactly what a recommender can extract from truthful, behaviour-grounded answers, with no recommenderderived quantity anywhere in the answer path, a property we enforce in code and state as a first-class part of the protocol (Section 6.4). What it does not measure is how a person phrases, hedges or misreads a rendered question. Human answer noise is a property of the elicitation layer and belongs to a study of that layer; conflating the two is how a simulator comes to flatter the system it is simulating. Four requirements. An instrument fit for measuring conversational elicitation must simultaneously satisfy four requirements, stated informally here and formally in Section 2. Frontier parity names a tolerance, fixed in advance at within 1.5% relative of the strongest measured baseline on both axes, not an equality. R2, an open set-valued input interface: it must ingest an evidence set of any cardinality, from zero upward, without peruser retraining, and the members of that set must be arbitrary embeddings rather than pre-committed ones. Any-length input alone is undemanding, since every recommender folds an arbitrary number of its own columns; the requirement is that a vector the model never saw when it was fitted, an out-of-catalogue concept, an entity, a phrase, a direction a policy emits, is a legal member of the set. R3, monotonicity: a truthful answer must not lower ranking quality beyond noise. R4, expressive answers: an answer must be able to say what a star rating says and an indicator cannot, that the user dislikes something and how strongly. R2 is an elicitation requirement rather than an engineering preference. A cold interview is rate-limited by what the user can actually answer, and answerability rises with abstraction: a question about a kind of film presupposes nothing, where a question about a particular film presupposes having seen it. An instrument restricted to catalogue items is therefore capped by the user’s viewing history; one restricted to a curated attribute list is capped by whoever wrote the list. Neither cap is a property of the user, and since abstraction has no natural ceiling, any vocabulary fixed at training time forecloses part of what a user could have told us, with which part decided before the user arrives. The bifurcation. The central empirical fact this article documents about the literature is that R1 and {R2, R3, R4} are anti-correlated across published systems. The models that hit the full-profile bar, closed-form linear autoencoders [49, 50], amortised VAEs [32, 47] and the training-free graph filters [10, 39, 46], are item-only point estimators on a onehot indicator basis. The models with a multi-channel elicitation interface, soft-attribute Gaussian elicitation [5], itemplus-attribute Thompson bandits [29], conjugate critique folds over a frozen factorisation [54] and per-item Bayesian natural-language elicitation [3], do not report full-profile accuracy at the R1 bar. We call these the accuracy corner and the elicitation corner, and we are aware of no published system that occupies both. Research questions. The article is organised around four questions. RQ1 Is the published ranking of cold-start question-selection heuristics a property of the heuristics, or of the heuristic– recommender pair? RQ2 Does any published recommender satisfy the four requirements an instrument for elicitation measurement must meet, and where does each candidate fall short? RQ3 Can a recommender satisfy all four requirements at the full-profile accuracy frontier, and what does each requirement cost? RQ4 What does a certified instrument make measurable that was not measurable before, and what does it show about the item and concept channels of a cold interview? Contributions. This article builds the recommender side of CASPER (Cold-start Adaptive Selection for Preference Elicitation in Recommendation). It does not claim a new elicitation policy; it claims the recommender that makes policy measurement possible. • We show that strategy orderings are instrument-relative: the same three heuristics, under the same protocol, are ranked three different ways by six consumers, and the classic consumer reproduces the classic published ordering while a frontier consumer inverts it, with every gap that decides one of the three orderings separated (RQ1). A recommender is statistically indistinguishable from a baseline on a metric if their difference’s paired per-user bootstrap confidence interval, on one shared harness (same users, items, split, metric depth), contains zero. This is a failure to separate, not a demonstration of equivalence: an interval containing zero is consistent with a small real difference in either direction that the test lacked the power to resolve, and we never read it as evidence that two systems are equally good. A number computed on a different metric or split (NDCG@10 on ML-25M against NDCG@100 on the ML-20M strong-generalisation split, say) is not comparable at all until bridged (Section 6.2). 2.2 How each requirement is checked Each requirement carries exactly one verification item. Two integrity controls apply across all four and exist because the per-requirement checks admit false positives. Table 4 in Section 7.2 states each criterion alongside what was measured against it. We deliberately do not require a high member lift after folding: the fold is trained to predict a user’s actual targets, and a user who likes film noir does not like every noir film; an operator that pushed all of a concept’s members up the ranking would be behaving as a genre filter. What we require instead is that the movement be specific: folding a like on a concept must move that concept’s members differently from folding a dislike on it, and differently again from folding some other answerable concept. R3, monotonicity. Starting cold, per-question full and tail NDCG@10 increase to budget on a question set fixed in advance, so the rise comes from answer content rather than from adaptive selection. Judged by the curve at budget rather than every-step significance, with the additional check that a single answer from cold already clears the no-answer intercept on both channels. R4, expressive answers. Sign: negating every answer lowers NDCG with a confidence interval excluding zero. Intensity: neutralising values to “indifferent” while freezing membership costs NDCG, and quality is monotone in the stated level. Graded input then beats a fair binarising ablation on two distinct comparisons, and we name what each varies: holding the fold-in support identical and re-valuing each item by its star level pays +0.0039 full at the full profile, while folding the graded history instead of the likes-only history, which adds the sub-threshold answers, pays far more on the short interview, +0.0102 at 𝑘=2, and nothing separable from 𝑘=8 onward. Integrity items, across all four: Existential controls. Wrong-user, shuffled and placebo-constant answers through the identical pipeline gain nothing relative to the true-answer arm (a control may legitimately fall below the no-answer intercept: a stranger’s taste is worse than no information, not equivalent to it), and duplicating an answer changes nothing, since the fold responds to information, not cardinality. No recommender geometry in answers. Every answer is computed from observed behaviour alone: an item answer is the user’s own rating; a concept answer is the log watch-lift log2((𝑛𝑐+ 1 2)/(𝑒𝑐+ 1 2)) formed from the user’s watched members 𝑛𝑐and from the count 𝑒𝑐expected under their volume and the concept’s catalogue popularity. Both are read from the user’s fold-in history; the held-out target half is excluded by construction, so an answer cannot encode its own target. No latent, no factor, no score and no quantity derived from the recommender enters an answer at any point, and the absence is asserted in code rather than assumed. Why the outcome checks alone are insufficient. R1 and R3 are outcome checks, and broken instruments pass both. A sign-blind fold that updates from only the direction of the queried entity but never the answer value still reproduces the base recommender (R1) and still beats a random-question control, because beating random certifies that a good question was selected, not that the answer was ingested; only R4’s sign flip and R3’s fixed-bank arm expose it. A circular answer model that generates answers from the instrument’s own latent geometry would inflate the R3 curve with no transferable signal. The risk is not hypothetical in this literature: evaluations commonly grant the simulated user privileged access to the target. PEBOL’s simulator answers from the ground-truth item description [3], and unified item-and-attribute conversational work assumes the user holds and reveals clear preferences over every attribute and item [13]. The answer-provenance rule closes this by construction rather than by measurement. We report both full and tail NDCG@10 for every check, because full-only or tail-only reporting hides an effect that is typically much larger on the tail than on the full catalogue. The reading is consistent across all of them: no family is strong on both the accuracy axis (R1) and the interview axis (R2–R4). The anchor of this section is the per-system master table (Table 1), which classifies every reasonable candidate against R1–R4 and, where a full-catalogue ranker exists, prints its full/tail NDCG@10 on our shared ML-25M protocol, so that the bifurcation is visible in measured numbers rather than asserted from published claims. Figure 1 plots the same fact. 3.1 Two protocols, and why both are reported A single protocol would beg the question, because the canonical one is not neutral between the families we are comparing. liang is the canonical protocol used throughout the full-profile literature and defined in Section 6: the fold-in is the user’s likes (𝑟> 3.5), and the candidate pool excludes those likes. It is the protocol every published number on this split was obtained under, and it is what makes our rows recognisable. But it discards a rating below the threshold entirely, so a “no” does not exist, which is exactly the signal the elicitation family is built to consume. Reporting that family only on liang would mean reporting it with its designed input removed. native evaluates each system under the input its own design assumes, and we report the outcome whether or not it favours that system. Each system receives its own published input contract: binary implicit feedback for the models whose papers specify it (which is why their two columns are identical: the input is byte-identical and only the pool moves), and the full rated history, signed and graded, for the ratings-native ones. To keep the comparison fair once the inputs differ, the candidate pool then excludes every rated fold-in item, likes and dislikes alike, for every system identically, regardless of what that system actually consumed. A model shown its own dislikes cannot collect a free lift by ranking them low, because they are already out of everyone’s pool. It is also the deployment-realistic choice, since a served system filters what the user has already rated. The two columns are therefore not a difficulty scale, and native numbers are uniformly higher for a reason that has nothing to do with capability: the larger exclusion hands back top-10 slots that a strong personalised model was spending on items the user had already seen. Most-Popular gains +0.028 from this alone and EASE +0.066, so the protocol systematically favours personalisation over popularity. Each column is read against its own rows, never a number in one against a number in the other. One system has no native cell. A native cell is admissible only where constructing it does not require optimising an objective this protocol contradicts. For every other system in the table, “native” names an input contract, and the protocol’s objective touches the system only at scoring. TaNP [33] is the exception: its published contribution is the training objective, episodic meta-learning for rating regression, so the two options are not comparable. Run it as published and it is optimising rating error, which is not the task this protocol scores; port its objective to ranking and it is no longer the published method. Three input channels have no faithful encoding in the itemindicator basis of every R1-bar model. (i) Graded and signed per-entity values: the basis has no sign and no magnitude, and a dislike is not representable as a negative one-hot without changing the likelihood. (ii) Concepts whose vocabulary is not fixed when the model is fitted: a column exists only for an entity present at fit time. (iii) Arbitrary continuous direction tokens. Folding these as linear observations in a frozen latent is the one leg of the conjunction we find nowhere in the literature. Three considerations tell against this. Most of the frontier has nothing to carry a belief over. A belief over a user latent needs a user vector in a dense latent and a direction in that latent to serve as a meaningful query. EASE has neither: its parameters are an item–item weight matrix and its “user” is the raw interaction row, one coordinate per catalogue item. There is no low-dimensional taste vector to be uncertain about and no direction to read 𝑤⊤Σ𝑤along. The same holds for EDLAE and for the training-free graph filters. For the closed-form family, most of the accuracy corner, the construction is not difficult, it is undefined. It is available to the amortised-encoder family, and that is where we build it. Where it is available, it carries a cost. Such a layer has to leave full-profile accuracy exactly intact, which is a property to be measured: our empty interview reproduces the frozen decoder’s ranking identically (Spearman 1.0000, |𝜇0| = 0), and the obvious alternative, ranking on a conjugate accumulator’s mean, forfeits roughly 0.12 full NDCG (Section 4). Nor does a dense latent accept concepts merely because it is dense: a raw concept centroid ranks its own member items at AUC 0.44, below chance, and only whitening makes it a direction (0.94). The channel is built, not inherited. And the reason to pay is that policy conclusions are not portable across recommenders. This is the argument for an instrument, and it is empirical. On a strong ingestion model, differences between question-selection strategies compress: a random question from an answerable bank matches popularity-ranked items at 𝑞=1, 2, 4, 8 and loses only at 𝑞=16; a one-level adaptive probe pays +0.0015 full (CI [+0.0007, +0.0024]); and an uncertainty-guided selector beats random by +0.0036 at the largest budget alone. A weaker recommender leaves more of the work to the question, so the same strategy gap measured there is larger and, if strategies are affected unequally, need not preserve the ordering. A policy result obtained on a weak recommender therefore carries an unknown amount of the recommender’s own behaviour, which is exactly the confound an instrument exists to remove, and Section 7.1 shows it doing so. On what the elicitation literature reports. Cold-start MRR over a hundred-item shortlist, top-|𝑆| agreement with a posterior, and turn counts to a success state all measure a system on a task it would not perform in service. Fullcatalogue NDCG is the only number in which an elicitation gain and a recommender weakness are on the same scale, and a policy improvement measured on a shortlist may not survive contact with 18,359 candidates. Makarova et al. 4 The Instrument The design follows from the gap analysis: use the shallow frozen recommender that already holds the accuracy corner (R1; the flat-since-2019 frontier means no deep-net detour is warranted), and add over its frozen latent a permutationinvariant set encoder that folds an evidence set of any length to a point (R2, R3), with every channel entering as a direction paired with a value (R4). Figure 2 summarises the interface. 4.1 The frozen base recommender The base model is a certified shallow recommender, our RecVAE realisation [47], trained once on the full catalogue and then frozen. It exposes (a) item factor rows Q𝑖∈ℝ𝑑, (b) a decoder read-out 𝑠𝑖(u) = Q⊤ 𝑖u + 𝛽𝑖with a learned bias 𝛽𝑖, and (c) the encoder that maps a full interaction set to a latent. The decoder, its item factors and its bias are never updated; what is trained over them is the set encoder described below, and the whole model is frozen once that training is complete. Freezing is the guarantee that R1 is a property of the base model, not of the fold placed over it. It is a design choice we share with the soft-attribute line: Göpfert et al. [20] are explicit that “we do not use the tag data when training the collaborative filtering model”, and Bıyık et al. [5] train the CF model and the attribute directions separately. We deliberately do not extend the linear basis with concept columns (that stays in the item-indicator world and fails R2, because the vocabulary would again be fixed when the model is fitted), nor retrain the recommender to accommodate a channel, which would put R1 back at risk for no stated gain. Architecture and training of the set encoder. The set encoder is a permutation-invariant network of the deep-sets form over the frozen 𝑑=200 latent. Each item observation enters as the item’s own embedding, modulated by a learned scale and shift indexed by its half-star level (ten levels, 0.5 to 5.0 stars). The modulated embeddings pass through a token network of width 512 and are sum-pooled. A residual network of the same width then maps three inputs to a shift of the latent: the pooled sum, the frozen RecVAE encoder’s mean on the liked tokens, and the answered count. The weight on that anchor is gated by the number of answered items. A separate branch of the same form reads items that were asked about but not rated, so that exposure is not encoded as a weak dislike, and a learned prior latent supplies the belief with no evidence. The residual network’s last layer, the exposure branch’s last layer and the prior are zero-initialised, so at initialisation the encoder is the frozen base model exactly and every departure from it has to be earned by the loss. The trainable parameters number 1.07 million against the frozen decoder. Training draws evidence sets from the training users’ fold-in histories under a curriculum. The curriculum mixes full profiles, dropout-thinned profiles, short interviews of at most eight answered items in which unanswerable questions arrive as refusal tokens, and empty or single-answer sets. The objective is the multinomial negative log-likelihood of each user’s held-out target items under the frozen decoder, with a smaller learning rate on the warm-started parameters than on the new branches. One run was trained; the checkpoint was selected on the validation users by full NDCG@10 and scored once on the test users. The selected model scores 0.3496/0.2491 full/tail on the held-out test cohort and reproduces the frozen decoder exactly at the empty interview (Section 7.2). It is this checkpoint that every channel below is folded into, and it is frozen for everything that follows. 4.2 Channel-agnostic observations: one representation, one form of observation Every channel supplies the same kind of thing. Critiquing is the one line that appears to accept free-form input and therefore appears to satisfy R2. It does not: across the family the critique is defined as a one-hot indicator over a closed set. DLC embeds a one-hot keyphrase [55]; LLC states that critiques “are encoded as one-hot keyphrase indicators” [36]; CE-VAE defines “a one-hot vector with keyphrase length 𝑠” [35]; M&Ms-VAE uses “a one-hot vector of length |𝐾|” [1]; Bot Play accumulates over a fixed aspect set [30]. The vocabularies are small, 40 or 75 keyphrases for catalogues of thousands of items, and where the representation is continuous, the continuity is downstream of an argmax. The lineage is older than the neural work and has the same shape: FindMe’s tweaks are fixed operations on a slot-value schema [7], and Chen and Pu’s survey defines a unit critique as one that “can only constrain over a single feature at a time” [8]. The nearest counterexample is BCIE, whose critique is a knowledge-graph fact drawn from about 57k entities and 46 relations [54], three orders of magnitude larger and the right place to hedge R2’s strength, but the fact must still be one the system surfaced. What none of these holds jointly is the conjunction this channel occupies: continuous, out-of-catalogue concept directions (a whitened centroid or a text-adapter image, with no per-attribute probe to train), driven by a signed, exposure-corrected implicit affinity (Eqs. That inflates every downstream number without transferring to a real user, and it cannot be detected from the curve, because a circular answer model produces a curve of exactly the shape a working one produces. We therefore treat the answer path as a protocol object, with a rule stated in advance and enforced in code. The rule. An answer may be computed only from the user’s fold-in behaviour and from catalogue-level counts. An item answer is the user’s own recorded rating; a concept answer is the signed log watch-lift of Eq. (2). Three things are excluded by construction, and each exclusion is asserted in code. No recommender-derived quantity: no latent, factor, score, similarity or model-predicted rating enters an answer, and the answer path never reads the recommender. No target-half information: answers are computed from the fold-in half only, so an answer cannot encode the held-out items it will be scored against. No oracle over unobserved preference: where a user has no evidence about a concept, the estimator emits a refusal rather than an imputed preference. Applied as a checklist, the rule separates this literature sharply. PEBOL’s simulated user answers from the groundtruth item description [3], so the answer is a function of the target. Unified item-and-attribute conversational work assumes the user holds and reveals a clear preference over every attribute and item [13], which supplies an oracle exactly where a cold user has no evidence. Bandit-over-arms formulations draw answers from the same latent space the recommender ranks in [29], circularity in its purest form. None of these is a careless choice; each is a reasonable simulator design. But each makes the resulting interview curve a statement about the simulator as much as about the method, and none would pass the rule above. We make no claim that our answers are what a person would say; the claim is narrower and checkable: whatever these answers are, they are computed independently of the model being evaluated. 6.5 Why “just add genre columns” is not an alternative The obvious objection to the concept channel is that a concept could simply be appended to an item-only model as an extra column, a centroid-of-members pseudo-item in EASE or an extra input unit in RecVAE. The objection fails for a structural reason. EASE’s item–item weight matrix is defined over a fixed column set and is obtained in closed form from the Gram matrix of exactly those columns; RecVAE’s input layer has one weight vector per column. Adding a concept therefore means adding a column, and adding a column means refitting the model: the set of expressible concepts is frozen at training time. A paraphrase of an existing concept, a concept invented after deployment, a direction produced by snapping a continuous query to the nearest meaningful axis, none can be answered without retraining, because none has a column. The pseudo-item is not merely a lossy encoding of a concept; it is a closed one. Report the consumer with the strategy. Table 3 shows that a strategy ordering measured through one recommender need not survive a change of recommender, and that the disagreement is not a symptom of weak models. An elicitation result should therefore name the recommender that consumed the answers, its full-profile standing on a shared protocol, and its input contract (binary, graded, signed); a reader can then tell whether a reported ordering is expected to transfer to their own system. Where the deployment recommender is known, the comparison should be run through it, and the ordering a tree induces should be believed by a designer whose production system is a tree. Stating what may enter an answer, and asserting it in code, is cheaper than any downstream control and is the one part of an interview evaluation a reader cannot reconstruct from the metrics alone. Certifying another consumer. The battery is portable in the sense that none of its items refers to this architecture. A team with its own recommender can run it as follows. Fix the strongest full-profile system available under one shared protocol and measure R1 against it, on both axes, with the identity check at the empty interview. With the consumer fixed and certified, the questions the elicitation literature is actually about become measurable on it: which questions to ask, in what order, from which channel, and whether adapting to answers pays over a well-chosen static order. The one-level probe of Section 7.2 shows that adaptivity is measurable here with a confidence interval, and the channel comparison of Section 7.3 shows that the comparison is criterion-relative. We stated the four requirements a recommender must meet to serve as an instrument for that literature. Under one shared protocol, the published literature is bifurcated between an accuracy corner and an elicitation corner, with no system in the conjunction (RQ2). We built one that occupies it: a frozen, certified recommender with a permutation-invariant set encoder over its latent space, into which items, out-of-catalogue concepts and free-text phrases fold as a direction paired with a value. The instrument reaches full/tail NDCG@10 of 0.3496/0.2491 against a RecVAE bar of 0.3540/0.2497, from implementations that reproduce the published ML-20M numbers to the third decimal. A What Restoring the Ratings Is Worth: The Full Decomposition The premise of the native column is that the threshold filter withholds the signal the ratings-native designs were built for. Restoring it helps one system and hurts another, and we are unable to attribute the difference to any property of the rating contract itself. The clearest loss is user-kNN, measured through one estimator and one code path on two inputs: 0.3756 on binary co-occurrence against 0.2822 on mean-centred ratings, a drop of −0.0934 full and −0.0976 tail. The clearest gain is iALS with ratings as confidence [22], 𝑐𝑢𝑖= 1 + 𝛼𝑟𝑢𝑖, which gains +0.0194 full NDCG@10 (CI [+0.0157, +0.0231]) over the best binary build of the same model (the +0.0170 in the caption of Table 1 is the same cell against the binary build scored under the native pool, a different comparator). Isolating that gain requires care, because the graded fold-in is 2.26× richer than the likes-only one, so a naive contrast confounds the values with the evidence volume and with the hyperparameters each build prefers. Holding one variable at a time on the same 10,000 users, every step separated in a paired bootstrap: re-selecting the hyperparameters on the reported metric is worth +0.0257 (CI [+0.0224, +0.0291]); folding the same additional rated items in with their values erased is worth −0.0088 (CI [−0.0125, −0.0052]); putting the true values back on that identical support recovers +0.0282 (CI [+0.0261, +0.0302]). More evidence, recorded as though every rating were a like, makes the model worse; the values, not the additional evidence, carry the gain. We use that fixed-support step to decompose and never as a headline, because a control that mislabels dislikes as likes is not the baseline any deployed positive-only system presents. The largest loss turns out to be configuration rather than anything about ratings. RBMF’s published recipe meancentres the targets, and under it the graded build trails the binary build by 0.070 full. With each arm’s ridge re-selected on validation and one frozen basis held, the same comparison is −0.0045 full (CI [−0.0069, −0.0022]) and +0.0046 tail (CI [+0.0021, +0.0071]). Both intervals exclude zero, so the effect is real on each axis and points in opposite directions, at a twentieth of the magnitude under the published recipe. The decomposition locates the difference: mean-centring the targets costs 0.0981 full on its own, and folding the sub-threshold items in with their values erased costs a further 0.0499, while restoring the true values on that identical support is worth +0.0454 (CI [+0.0428, +0.0479]). The centring, not the rating contract, accounts for the loss. Three of the ratings-native rows, iALS, RBMF and the conjugate belief, shared one hyperparameter setting selected by a validation sweep that optimised NDCG@100 on a previous dataset, while this article reports NDCG@10. Reselecting on the reported metric moves RBMF by +0.0601 full in this column, iALS by +0.0281 and belief-MF by +0.0267, and RBMF’s own regularisation, not the shared basis, accounts for most of its gain. The consequence is the one stated in Section 3.2: restoring the published rating contract does not reliably improve these systems, the one system it clearly helps uses ratings as confidence in a plain factorisation, and one row shows what the column was built to detect, a system that reads its own contract and improves.
