Off-Catalogue Elicitation Directions}\\[4pt]\large Thesis Chapter C (v2) --- working draft}
\author{}
\date{August 2026}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent A cold-start interview can only ask what is in its question catalogue; this chapter takes
seriously the case where the direction the data says to ask about is not in it. A policy trained on
gain-weighted blends of the candidate questions whose value was \emph{measured} at each interview
state emits directions that lie on no catalogue entry, while still running the tree's ordinary
interview (fit cosine $0.970$; $245$ of $245$ distinct questions preserved) --- at the states where
the question landscape was measured, and not beyond them. A small adapter into a frozen language
model says such a direction in English, and the vector genuinely conditions the text: against a
correspondence-scrambled twin trained identically, $+0.111$ $[+0.105,\,+0.117]$ through an
independently fitted bridge, at $107\%$ of the true phrase's own ceiling. Where the decoder is
distinctive the rendered word lands at median rank $91$ of $1{,}031$ among the direction's own
nearest concepts --- while the \emph{nearest catalogue question's} own text is anti-aligned with that
direction ($-0.158$) where the invented word carries it ($+0.204$). Translating a direction into
English and reading it back costs $+0.018$ NDCG@10 under an answer model whose circularity we
declare and from which we read only the difference between arms, and most of that cost is the
decoder repeating itself rather than language. The claim is capability, not value: what is
unoccupied is the conjunction --- a \emph{collaborative} latent, rendered as \emph{language}, left
\emph{unsnapped}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A cold-start recommender that interviews its user has to ask something, and everything it can ask is
on a list. In this thesis the list has $19{,}390$ entries --- $18{,}359$ films and $1{,}031$
concepts --- and Chapters~A and~B are largely about choosing well from it: which entry splits this
cohort, which one a person can actually answer, which order pays. The list is the action space, and
an action space is a comfortable thing to have. Everything is enumerable, everything is
answerable-by-construction, and the hard part reduces to an argmax.

This chapter is about the moment that comfort breaks. Three questions into an interview --- no answer
to \emph{do you like better-than-expected films?}, no answer on \emph{True Lies}, a dislike for
\emph{Apollo 13} --- the policy's own machinery computes a direction it would like to probe, and then
does the only thing it can do: it snaps that direction to the closest thing on the list and asks that
instead. How much is lost in the snap is an empirical question, and it turns out to be
measurable. At the interview states we study, the nearest catalogue entry is close in the policy's
own head space (cosine $0.93$) --- and yet \textbf{that entry's own text points the wrong way}. Pushed
through a bridge fitted independently of everything else in this chapter, the nearest catalogue
question's gold phrase is anti-aligned with the direction the policy emitted, at $-0.158$
$[-0.224,\,-0.089]$, while a word invented on the spot by a small language model reading that same
direction carries it at $+0.204$ $[+0.117,\,+0.291]$ (two caveats travel with that gap and are
stated beside it in \S\ref{sec:c4}). The direction is expressible in English. The catalogue simply
does not contain the sentence that expresses it.

That single contrast is what this chapter is for, and the rest is the work of earning the right to
show it. Four steps. First (\S\ref{sec:c0}), we look at what the deployed policy actually emits
before its snap, and find a vector that never once in $160{,}000$ interview states lies on the
question it selects (maximum cosine $0.1203$) --- though for a duller reason than it sounds, which we
report rather than exploit. Second (\S\ref{sec:c1}), we retrain the policy on targets that are
gain-weighted blends of the candidates whose value was measured at each state, so that off-catalogue
emission is taught rather than hoped for; one object then does both jobs, running the tree's ordinary
interview with $245$ of $245$ distinct questions intact while fitting its blend targets at cosine
$0.970$. Third (\S\ref{sec:c3}), we train a two-layer adapter to say a direction in English through a
frozen \texttt{distilgpt2}, and test the only thing that matters about it --- whether the text depends
on the vector --- against a twin trained identically on scrambled vector--text pairs
($+0.111$ $[+0.105,\,+0.117]$, with the true arm at $107\%$ of what the ground-truth phrase itself
earns through the same instrument, and source identification at $0.678$). Fourth
(\S\ref{sec:c4}), we close the loop: the rendered word lands at median rank $91$ of $1{,}031$ in the
direction's own neighbourhood where the decoder says something distinctive --- \texttt{action} at rank
$1$, \texttt{indictment} at $5$ for a courtroom blend, \texttt{animals} at $7$ for the Pixar branch
--- and we measure what the round trip through language costs.

\paragraph{What is claimed, as a conjunction.} Every leg of this has prior art or a near-miss, and we
claim no leg on its own. Generating objects the catalogue does not contain is GeneRec's
territory~\cite{wang2023generec}; verbalising a synthesised latent point is ELM's
mechanism~\cite{tennenholtz2023elm} and we borrow it rather than invent one; synthesising a query
continuously and then snapping it to real items was done inside recommendation six years
ago~\cite{vendrov2020gradient}. What is unoccupied is the conjunction of three things: the decoded
point is \textbf{chosen by an interview policy at an interview state}, the renderer is audited
against a \textbf{correspondence-scrambled} latent rather than by round-trip cosine or a plausibility
panel, and faithfulness is scored \textbf{causally}, by substituting the text where the latent was
and pushing it through an independently fitted map. The nearest published relative sharpens this
rather than blunting it. EAGLE steers a frozen language model toward high-utility regions of a
MovieLens-25M behavioural embedding~\cite{tennenholtz2024eagle}, and it has no decoder at all: it
edits text and encodes the result, so no latent point is ever mapped to language, there is no user
and no question, and its authors name the price themselves --- coverage is limited by the action
space, and they see no remedy that would guarantee coverage of the embedding space. They looked at
decoding an off-manifold point and routed around it. We decode, and then we audit what comes out.

\paragraph{Two things we say here rather than in a limitations paragraph.} The claim is about
\textbf{capability, not value}. We show that a direction outside the bank can be emitted, said, and
audited; we do not show --- and with the answer model available to us cannot show --- that asking it
would improve anyone's recommendations, and no version of that sentence appears in this chapter.
Chapter~B is the reason this is a considered position rather than a hedge: there, enlarging the
question bank made the selectors that consume it perform \emph{worse}. And the scope of the emission
result is narrow by measurement, not by modesty: \S\ref{sec:c1} withholds a subtree and finds that
the policy fits the states it was shown and lands on the wrong side of zero at the states it was
not, with the one statistic that appeared to show otherwise matched by the \emph{untrained} network.
So the sentence this chapter is licensed to write is ``the policy emits where the question landscape
was measured'', and it is written that way everywhere. Separately, the one measurement here that
needs somebody to answer a question --- what the round trip through English costs --- runs on a
simulated geometric answerer, which is circular; we declare that in our own voice, put both arms
under the same simulator, and read only the difference between them. Every other reading in this
chapter is a property of a vector or of a string, never the model grading its own answer.

\paragraph{Structure.} \S\ref{sec:positioning} states the claim, the thirty-four-year-old
answerability problem it inherits, and what each neighbouring system does and does not do.
\S\ref{sec:setup} fixes the instrument and the statistical conventions. \S\ref{sec:c0}--\S\ref{sec:c4}
are the empirical arc. \S\ref{sec:limitations} collects what remains open --- including the one
human study we argue \emph{for}, on answerability rather than on quality --- and \S\ref{sec:conclusion}
closes.

\section{Positioning}
\label{sec:positioning}

\subsection{The claim, and the thing it is not}

The claim of this chapter is that \textbf{the policy emits off-catalogue directions at the interview
states where the question landscape was measured, a trained renderer can say them in English, the
text --- unlike text from a correspondence-scrambled latent --- reproduces the direction's causal
effect, and the rendered word lands in the direction's own semantic neighbourhood where the nearest
catalogue question's text points the other way.} The scope clause in that sentence is load-bearing
and \S\ref{sec:c1} is where it is earned rather than assumed.

That is a claim about \textbf{capability, not value}, and the distinction is not modesty. Chapter B
established, on this same instrument, that the action space was not the constraint: enlarging the
question bank made the selectors that consume it perform \emph{worse}, not better. A chapter that
went on to argue that a still richer action space --- one unbounded by any bank --- is what the
interview was missing would be arguing with the thesis it belongs to. So we do not argue it. We show
that a direction outside the bank can be emitted, said, and audited; we do not show, and given our
answer model cannot show, that asking it would improve a recommendation.

Two consequences follow, and we state them before any result rather than in a limitations paragraph.
First, \textbf{we make no claim about the value of the emitted point at all}. An earlier draft of
this chapter argued that the emission target's construction --- a gain-weighted blend of measured
candidates --- \emph{bounds} the emitted point's value by a convex combination of measured question
values. That argument is withdrawn: it is a statement about vectors asserted as a statement about
value, and it has no affineness to stand on. What survives is the construction itself, and it is
worth stating precisely because it is weaker than it sounds. Every gradient in \S\ref{sec:c1} comes
from a candidate whose value we measured; we claim nothing about the value of the blend point; and
the ingredient set at a given state is an unstable draw from a near-flat measured gain profile, whose
\emph{direction} is only moderately stable. \S\ref{sec:c1} reports that instability rather than
assuming it away. Second, the simulated answerer that puts the
policy at an interview state is a \textbf{state generator}, and it is scored through in exactly one
place. Every faithfulness reading in \S\ref{sec:c2}--\S\ref{sec:c4} is a property of the point or of
the text, never an outcome of simulating an answer and observing that the belief improved --- that
would be the model answering its own question and grading itself. The single exception is the
translation-cost measurement of \S\ref{sec:c4}, which needs an answer model by construction, declares
its circularity in the paper's own voice, puts both arms under the same simulator, and reads only the
\emph{difference}. No absolute row from it is headlined and no version of ``asking the synthesised
question improves recommendations'' is claimed anywhere in this chapter.

Finally, one piece of territory is already claimed and we narrow to fit it. Generating objects
outside the corpus is not an unoccupied idea in recommendation: GeneRec proposes an explicit AI
creator for items the catalogue does not contain~\cite{wang2023generec}, and EAGLE's reward carries
an explicit distance-from-nearest-neighbours novelty term~\cite{tennenholtz2024eagle}. Our object is
off-catalogue in the \textbf{question channel}, where the thing emitted must be answered rather than
consumed. We claim the channel, never the trick.

\subsection{Answerability: a thirty-four-year-old failure, and the two standard fixes}

Synthesising a query rather than selecting one is not new, and its canonical result is negative. Baum
and Lang showed in 1992 that a learner which synthesises the maximally informative input and asks a
human to label it can work poorly for a reason no amount of better optimisation
fixes~\cite{baum1992query}: the informative point is a fusion of two classes --- a hybrid digit ---
and a person cannot label it, so the query returns noise rather than information. We cite the claim
and quote no number from it; the 1992 proceedings text was not obtainable.

The modern reproduction is the citable evidence. Zhu and Bento's generative adversarial active
learning synthesises instances with a GAN and has \emph{human oracles} label
them~\cite{zhu2017gaal}; their own text records that labellers ``failed to confidently identify most
of the generated cat and dog images'', and --- the part that matters --- the authors narrowed their
experiments to easy binary pairs because of it. A fluent generative renderer did not buy
answerability. That is the burden this chapter takes on, and it is why the load-bearing evidence here
is a control rather than a demonstration of fluency.

The field's two standard responses to Baum and Lang are exactly the two options in front of us:
\textbf{snap the synthesised point to a real neighbour}, or \textbf{verbalise it}. The first is the
majority answer and it recurs independently in at least four literatures --- local membership queries
in learning theory, nearest-neighbour query synthesis in active learning, real-attribute profiles in
polyhedral conjoint analysis, and, inside recommendation itself, the projection step of Vendrov et
al.~\cite{vendrov2020gradient} \todo{bib keys for the local-membership-query and NN-query-synthesis
citations are not yet in references.bib; either add them or drop this list to Vendrov alone}. Removing
the snap is therefore the risk we are taking, not a shortcut we are declining, and four fields
concluded it is not safe in general. The second response --- verbalise --- has one published instance
close to ours: GenMQD generates a textual description of the wanted instance instead of the instance
itself, and reports a user study in which human oracles prefer the text queries to generated image
queries~\cite{genmqd2024}. It is vision active learning, where the human acts by fetching matching
real data rather than by answering a question about themselves, so it does not occupy our cell --- but
it does mean ``render the synthesised query as text'' is not an idea we may claim.

\subsection{Where synthesised queries do reach people, and why not here}

The unqualified sentence ``nobody puts a synthesised continuous query to a user'' is false, and it is
false in a way that sharpens rather than weakens the claim. Wherever the design can be
\emph{physically executed}, this is routine. LineCoSpar maintains a posterior over a utility function
on a continuous six-dimensional gait-parameter space, proposes a gait that comes from no pool, has an
exoskeleton walk it, and collects a preference from a real subject --- six able-bodied subjects, thirty
learning trials each, at most twenty-nine pairwise preferences and thirty pieces of coactive feedback
per subject~\cite{tucker2020linecospar}. Adaptive design optimisation does the same in psychophysics
with real participants across three populations~\cite{ahn2020ado}. In both, answerability is free
because the world renders the design: the question \emph{is} the stimulus.

Recommendation has no such renderer. A point in a collaborative-filtering latent cannot be walked,
displayed or tasted, which is precisely why the field snapped to a catalogue. The proposal of this
chapter is that \textbf{language is the missing renderer}, and the reason the proposal needs an audit
rather than a demonstration is that language, unlike an exoskeleton, will render anything --- including
a vector with no meaning at all.

\subsection{The neighbours, and what each one lacks}

\paragraph{ELM --- the rendering mechanism, without anything choosing what to render.} An adapter maps
vectors from a frozen recommendation model into a language model's token-embedding space, and the
language model describes the vector; the vectors described include synthesised ones ---
interpolations between two films, interpolations between two users, extrapolations along concept
directions --- and consistency metrics are introduced precisely because such points have no ground
truth~\cite{tennenholtz2023elm}. We take this mechanism rather than invent one, and we say so: an LLM verbalising a
synthesised latent point is \textbf{not} a contribution of this chapter. What ELM has no notion of is
\emph{selection}. Every vector it decodes is precomputed or analyst-supplied; there is no interview,
no state, no policy choosing which point to decode, and no user whose answer comes back. The adapter
injects the domain vector as a \emph{single} token embedding; longer sequences are named in its own
footnote as future work. Its validation is round-trip re-embedding cosine, a downstream ranking
correlation, and a hundred human raters. It does report an output-side random baseline --- a
random-ranking floor and a ground-truth ceiling on the same axis --- so the narrow and correct
statement is that there is \textbf{no input-side embedding null}: no condition in which the vector
fed to the renderer is scrambled or random. Two further details of that validation are worth stating
because they are the reason we do not reuse it. Semantic consistency re-embeds the generated text
with the very dual-encoder that produced the input vector, so the metric is closed over the system's
own components; and in the extrapolation experiment closest to our use case, behavioural consistency
is scored against the collaborative-filtering model's own \emph{predicted} ratings. In that
experiment behavioural consistency \emph{increases} the further the user embedding is pushed along a
concept direction, which the authors attribute to the profile becoming more predictable for the model
that scores it --- a published, first-party instance of a faithfulness score moving the wrong way
off-manifold. ELM also states that it does not evaluate arbitrary points at all: only interpolations
of existing entities and shifts from them.

\paragraph{EAGLE --- the published propose-and-encode loop, and our motivation in its authors' own
words.} EAGLE trains an RL agent to steer a frozen language model toward high-utility regions of a
MovieLens-25M behavioural embedding~\cite{tennenholtz2024eagle}. It is the nearest published relative
of a policy that optimises in a recommender's latent space and whose output is language, and it
kills any unqualified version of that sentence: the surviving qualifiers are \emph{decode},
\emph{interview}, and \emph{question}. Three facts separate it from this chapter, all taken from its
own text. \textbf{It has no decoder} --- it requires only an encoder, edits text and encodes the
result, so no latent point is ever mapped to language; the architecture is propose-and-encode, and
our variant-(a) arm in \S\ref{sec:c3} is that architecture minus the RL, not a strawman we built.
\textbf{There is no user and no question} --- the user embedding is fixed historical
collaborative-filtering output, nothing is asked, no answer is observed, and nothing is folded back
into the embedding space. \textbf{Its automatic evaluation is its own reward function}, computed by
the encoder that defines the objective the agent was trained to maximise; its only evidence not
routed through the reward is a panel of $124$ post-hoc raters who never see the latent. It does
report a uniform reference policy over its action set, so we never write that it reports no random
baseline --- what it does not report is a scrambled-latent or mismatched-user condition.

Its authors also state our motivation better than we could. They identify the off-manifold problem
explicitly --- a latent point deemed optimal may not map to any valid entity --- and achieve
realisability by construction, by never leaving text; and they name the price, that coverage is
inherently limited by the action space and that no remedy they consider would guarantee coverage of
the embedding space. The nearest published relative looked at decoding an off-manifold optimal point
and routed around it. That is the gap this chapter walks into, with its eyes open.

\paragraph{Vendrov et al.\ --- synthesis-then-snap, inside recommendation, at AAAI 2020.} The query
optimisation relaxes the item set by allowing attributes to vary continuously without requiring that
they correspond to available items, and then projects the resulting hypothetical items back to the
nearest real ones~\cite{vendrov2020gradient}. This is genuine query synthesis followed by a snap, in
our own field, six years ago, and it forecloses ``optimise the query continuously'' as a
contribution. Our claim is therefore stated against the three things it does not do: the space is a
\textbf{collaborative} latent rather than an attribute space, the hypothetical is \textbf{rendered as
language}, and it is left \textbf{unsnapped}. Their partial comparison queries --- specifying only a
subset of attributes, explicitly to reduce the user's cognitive burden --- are also prior art for
asking about part of a direction, and we check against them before presenting any sparse rendering as
new.

\paragraph{Info-Synth --- our design, minus the three things this chapter is about.} Info-Synth
maximises a mutual-information objective over a continuous query space to synthesise a pairwise
query, uses the synthesised query directly in the unconstrained setting, and snaps to dataset items
only where the domain requires it~\cite{nadagouda2026infosynth}. It is the closest live neighbour and
the natural foil for the whole chapter, and the absence we point at has to be stated narrowly,
because response quality is its headline contribution rather than its blind spot. Info-Synth models
the reliability of a comparison as a closed-form function of the query's \emph{geometry} --- the
distances from the user point to the two synthesised points, through a heteroscedastic probit with a
single noise scale --- and never as a function of whether the synthesised query denotes anything a
respondent could be shown; nothing in that model refers to the content of either point. The
experimental version of the same observation is sharper and is checkable line by line: in the one
semantically embedded space they study, text summaries encoded with a language model, the synthesised
query is never posed --- it is snapped back onto real summaries before any response is obtained. The
continuous setting, in which the synthesised pair is used directly, is exercised only on uniform
random synthetic vectors and on robot controller gains, both of which are instantiable by simulation.
No human answers any query in the paper: the synthetic and robotics studies use an automated oracle,
and the text experiments replay preference labels collected years earlier over pairs that already
existed. So the question of how a synthesised point in a semantic space would be presented to a
respondent does not arise in their experiments. Info-Synth treats answer \emph{noise}; Baum and Lang's
problem is answer \emph{impossibility}, and this line does not name it. That gap is the reason our
answerability section opens in 1992 rather than in 2026. We therefore replicate Info-Synth on our
latent \textbf{answer-free only} --- synthesise the pair, measure where it lands and what the snap
costs --- and report no payoff for it: its response model \emph{is} its acquisition objective, so an
arm in which we simulated the answers could not fail and would be a self-consistency check dressed as
an experiment.

\paragraph{DeCap --- why the soft snap is justified with a number rather than an assertion.} The
tempting justification for snapping is that no decoder exists for an off-manifold point. That
justification is no longer safe: zero-shot embedding inverters are strong, and one of them reaches
round-trip cosine above $90$ while its F1 against the true text is only above
$50$~\cite{zhang2025zsinvert}. The honest
justification is measured, and it is DeCap's~\cite{li2023decap}. All four numbers below are CIDEr on
the MSCOCO Karpathy test split, zero-shot, with the decoder trained on CC3M text only, read off one
table so the comparison is controlled. Feeding a genuinely off-manifold vector to that text-trained
decoder --- in their case the raw CLIP \emph{image} embedding, off-manifold through the modality gap
rather than through synthesis --- collapses CIDEr to $8.1$; a hard nearest-neighbour snap to the
support memory recovers $27.1$, slightly above decoder-free CLIP retrieval, which uses no decoder at
all and returns the highest-similarity caption verbatim for $25.6$; and a softmax-weighted \emph{blend}
over that same memory reaches $42.1$. The ordering --- soft blend $\gg$ hard snap $>$ decoder-free
retrieval $\gg$ raw off-manifold vector --- is the argument for our blended emission target, published
in captioning, with numbers, and it reproduces on their other two benchmarks. The $25.6$ is a
retrieval floor, not a fourth decoding strategy, and we do not present it as a rung of the ladder.

\subsection{The validation gap, in one paragraph}

\begin{sloppypar}
The methodological half of this chapter is one control, stated narrowly: \textbf{we are aware of no
embedding-verbalisation system that reports what its renderer produces from a
correspondence-scrambled latent.} We checked eleven systems that have a renderer at all and found
the same toolkit throughout --- round-trip re-embedding cosine, a downstream task, and human
ratings. We do not write that nobody uses a random control or that nobody validates verbalisation
faithfulness; both are false, and the two fields that do run this control are the reason we run it.
Heap et al.\ trained sparse autoencoders on randomly initialised transformers and found automated
interpretability scores equal to or higher than for the trained model --- on Pythia-6.9b under
`fuzzing' scoring, trained $0.79$ against $0.87$ for both re-randomised variants and $0.88$ at
initialisation --- while the only null that separated sat at chance, $0.50$, and was the one that
replaced the token embeddings with i.i.d.\ Gaussian draws so that a token has no consistent
vector~\cite{heap2026autointerp}. Their strong null is itself Gaussian, so the separating axis is
not Gaussian-versus-not but \textbf{what is randomised: scramble the correspondence, not the
weights} --- and their scale dependence (Pythia-70m: $0.50$ and $0.49$ against a trained $0.63$) is
why the control has to be run at our operating scale rather than imported. Tang et al.'s fMRI
language decoder builds the same null by permuting voxel responses so that response statistics
survive and the response-to-stimulus correspondence does not~\cite{tang2023semantic}. The
construction is therefore not ours to claim; its \textbf{application} is --- to a rendered
elicitation question, where the validated object is a question a user would have to answer and where,
unlike a decoded transcript, no paired reference text exists. The metric we import is Hewitt et al.'s
causal one: substitute the text back where the latent was and measure how much of the latent's effect
it reproduces, normalised by an external ruler so that the latent is an arm rather than the ruler
itself~\cite{hewitt2025neologism}. This is also our answer to the reviewer request we expect. The
field's human instrument is a plausibility panel --- a hundred raters for ELM, a hundred and
twenty-four for EAGLE, an expert discrimination test for ctELM in which synthetic abstracts fool
clinicians $44\%$ of the time~\cite{ondov2026ctelm} --- and a good confabulator maximises that number,
because the rater never sees the source vector. \S\ref{sec:c3} reports the opposite failure in our own
space: machine-only synonyms, text a human would grade wrong that reproduces the vector's effect
better than the true surface form does.
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{What is unoccupied, stated as a conjunction}

No leg of the following is claimed on its own, and we claim none of them on their own. What is
unoccupied is the conjunction of three things: \textbf{(i)} the decoded point is chosen by an
interview policy at an interview state --- ELM has nothing selecting which vector to decode, EAGLE has
no interview; \textbf{(ii)} the renderer is audited against a \textbf{correspondence-scrambled}
latent, which none of the eleven systems we checked reports; and \textbf{(iii)} faithfulness is
scored \textbf{causally}, by plug-in substitution through an independently fitted map, where ELM's
and EAGLE's evidence is round-trip cosine and post-hoc raters. Each leg alone has prior art or a
near-miss. The three together are the contribution, and the sentence about a question nobody has a
word for is decoration on it, not a substitute for it.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{The instrument, the policy, and the contract --- in the form the rest of the chapter uses
them.} Nothing in this chapter is trained except where a section says so, and nothing in the
instrument is refitted at all: the recommender checkpoint, the fold-in operator and the question bank
are the certified artifacts of Chapters~A and~B, held frozen. The bank contains $19{,}390$ questions
in two channels --- $18{,}359$ items and $1{,}031$ concepts --- and the ruler is full and tail NDCG@10
on the canonical split's $10{,}000$ validation users. The policy is \textbf{E1}, Chapter~B's distilled
parametric policy: a network that reads the interview state, emits a continuous query vector, and asks
whichever bank question scores highest against it, trained by cross-entropy toward the tree's one-hot
pick and certified as reproducing that pick at top-1 agreement $1.0000$ at every position.

\section{C0: does the emitted point sit anywhere but on a bank question?}
\label{sec:c0}

E1 emits a continuous query vector at every interview state and then asks the bank question that
scores highest against it; this section captures that vector, $\zhat$, \emph{before} the snap, at all
$160{,}000$ states on which E1 was scored ($10{,}000$ validation users $\times$ $16$ positions), and
measures where it sits relative to the $19{,}390$ questions it is scored against. Nothing is trained
and nothing is sampled: the states are regenerated through the policy's own rollout path, the vector
is taken by a forward hook on the head, and every state is scored against every bank entry.

The unit those $160{,}000$ rows are counted in is load-bearing and we declare it before the first
number. The rows are $10{,}000$ users $\times$ $16$ \emph{nested} positions, and they resolve to
$31{,}874$ distinct asked-history states --- one at the root, three at the second position, and so on
to $6{,}153$ at the last --- inside $6{,}836$ distinct realised interviews. \textbf{$99.95\%$ of the
variance in $\cos_1$ is between states} rather than between users, at no less than $99.9\%$ at every
position, so the emitted vector is essentially a function of the discrete answer history and not of
the individual user. Every distribution below is therefore reported user-row-weighted, with the
state-weighted value given as a robustness row wherever it changes the reading, and intervals come
from a cluster bootstrap with the user as the cluster.

One detail of the head is load-bearing enough to state rather than absorb. The score is
\begin{equation*}
s(q \mid \text{state}) \;=\; \langle u(h_{\text{state}}),\, k(\varphi_q)\rangle \;+\; b(\varphi_q),
\end{equation*}
with a \textbf{per-candidate bias}, so a bare bilinear inner product is not the rule the policy
maximises and a geometry measured in that bare space would describe a scoring function E1 does not
use. We therefore measure in homogeneous coordinates, $\zhat = [\,u(h),\,1\,]$ and $e_q =
[\,k(\varphi_q),\,b(\varphi_q)\,]$, so that $\langle \zhat, e_q\rangle = s(q\mid\text{state})$
exactly; this is an identity, not an approximation, and it is asserted against the head's own forward
at run time (maximum absolute discrepancy $3.05\times10^{-4}$ on scores of magnitude $\approx 80$).
The emitted vector is therefore $129$-dimensional. The bias-free bilinear variant is measured
alongside and changes nothing that follows (median cosine $-0.179$ against $-0.182$).

\subsection{The mechanism}

The negative cosine is not a harness fault, and the explanation also disposes of the apparent
contradiction between the two registered statistics --- a softmax with one obvious winner over a
direction that points nowhere near it. \textbf{The whole score vector sits far below zero}: the
median top score is $-82.7$, and $95.2\%$ of states have a negative top score, while the decision
itself is made by a comparatively small variation on top of that --- median $s_1 - s_2 = 7.81$
logits, median $s_1 - s_{10} = 17.50$, against $\lvert s_1\rvert \approx 83$. Cross-entropy
constrains only score \emph{differences}, so nothing in E1's training penalises a large common-mode
component shared across candidates, and the head placed one there. In the raw space the scale control
looks damning: a random direction, which by construction knows nothing about this bank, is
\emph{closer} in cosine to whatever it happens to snap to ($+0.064$) than the policy is to the
question it deliberately selects ($-0.182$).

That comparison is an artifact of the common mode, and the diagnostic that removes it changes the
interpretation rather than the numbers. Subtracting the bank's mean direction --- which is enormous:
the mean pairwise cosine over the whole $19{,}390$-entry bank is $0.964$, and it falls to $0.0018$
once that one shared component is removed --- the emission's cosine to the entry it selects has median
$\mathbf{+0.488}$, IQR $[0.369,\,0.587]$, positive at $98.9\%$ of rows, against $\mathbf{+0.170}$ for
a norm-matched random direction scored against the same entries. So the corrected mechanism statement
is a sum, not a dismissal: \textbf{the trained emission is a large shared common-mode component,
which raw cosine sees, plus a genuine bank-aligned informative direction, which raw cosine hides. It
is a scoring functional over the bank rather than a semantic point in question space \emph{in the raw
space}; mean-removed, it is directionally meaningful, while still far from lying \emph{on} its
question ($0.49 \ll 1$) and still surrounded by near-ties.}

\subsection{What this licenses}

What is established is that $\zhat$ never sits on its snapped question --- the maximum cosine over
every row is $0.1203$ --- and, as a description rather than a rule-backed verdict, that the runners-up
are spread widely enough that the top gap is under half the top-ten range, most of all late in the
interview and in the out-of-pool region. That second statement is user-row-weighted; weighted by
distinct state the margin sits at $0.286$ against a random floor of $0.267$, which is the version a
sceptical reader should hold. What is \emph{not} established is that $\zhat$ carries useful
off-catalogue content. A vector can be far from every bank entry, and between several of them, for
the mundane reason that nothing in the loss ever had a reason to put it anywhere in particular: the
one-hot target constrains a comparison, and C0 measured the emission rather than the training signal.
Nor does a low margin ratio mean the interview is uncertain in any decision-relevant sense --- the
softmax is degenerate at the median state, and E1's realised interviews remain exactly the tree's.

That is also the sense in which this section hands over to the next. \textbf{C1's blend target is
therefore not merely a way to teach in-between emission; it is the step that would give $\zhat$ a
target in the question space, and a renderer fed E1's raw emission would be verbalising a vector
whose raw-space orientation is dominated by an offset.} The mean-removed diagnostic is what stops
that sentence being stronger: there is a real bank-aligned direction underneath the offset, so the
counterfactual is about what a renderer would be handed, not a proof that the emission is empty. The
distributions above, together with the random-direction floor, are the pre-C1 reference against which
\S\ref{sec:c1}'s ``the emissions moved off the grid'' is measured; they are not themselves evidence
for it.

\section{C1: a policy that emits off-catalogue directions, and does not extrapolate}
\label{sec:c1}

The design replaces E1's one-hot distillation target with a gain-weighted blend of the top-$m$
candidates whose value was measured at that interview state, so the target is off-catalogue by
construction while every gradient still comes from a question we actually measured. This section
reports \textbf{C1-final}, run on Paper~B's cold tree with persisted per-state gains: $72$ emission
states, trained jointly with a replay cross-entropy toward the tree's ordinary picks so that the
object remains a policy. It is validation- and train-side only; the $10{,}000$ canonical test users
were not touched.

\paragraph{The predecessor, and what it could not test.} An earlier pilot fine-tuned the same head to
$42$ blend targets with no replay term. It answered the capability question --- the architecture can
place a stable point off the grid, with the same off-grid signature as its target --- and it could
answer nothing else, because away from its $42$ states it stopped being a policy: snap agreement
$0.0000$ against the banked E1 in all six runs, and four to six distinct questions where E1 emits
$163$. It also could not test whether the blend was \emph{learned}: its registered same-nearest-bank-
question criterion was passed by the shuffled control at the same $33/42$, and the one statistic that
separated (a paired sign test, $30/42$, $p=0.0079$, full arm only) is a statement about fitting $42$
constants with $526$k parameters. C1-final supersedes every number in it.

\subsection{What is established, and what may not be claimed}

The emissions are genuinely off the grid: median target $\cos_1$ $0.894$ and median top-1 blend
weight $\mathbf{0.2254}$, with $0$ of $72$ states above $0.5$, against a random floor of $0.121$;
within-state per-user agreement is $0.99991$, so the emitted point at a state is one object rather
than an average over its cohort. The contract held throughout: the TRAIN snap constant
$0.15830706760250668$, the $1{,}004$-entry $\varphi$ bank asserted, $72/72$ gain sweeps matching the
banked splitter and splitter gain at maximum error $0$, and the descent reproducing all
$1{,}279{,}335$ banked labels with zero mismatches. Three hyperparameters (3 epochs, batch $512$,
lr $3\times10^{-4}$) were chosen once and never swept; the per-epoch curve
$0.850 \to 0.971 \to 0.977$ shows the fit converged, so epochs are not what binds reading~(a).

We may not claim that the policy emits sensible directions at arbitrary interview states, and we
quote no number from a held-out-state emission --- those are both off-target and out of scale, so
under C4's ruling~2 they would not be rendered at all. Nor do we claim that the blend's five
ingredients are the right five: $43$ of the $72$ cohorts sit below $500$ users, and split-half
stability is \emph{not computable} on this tree because no per-user gain sidecar exists, so the
pilot's mean-removed split-half direction cosine of $0.656$ is carried as a labelled prior and
nothing more.

\subsection{What the blend target is made of, and how stable it is}

The word ``measured'' in ``a blend of measured candidates'' has to be qualified, and the
qualification is ours rather than a reviewer's. On the pilot's $35$ deep-sweep states, under a
per-user bootstrap, the top-five ingredient set has \textbf{mean overlap $1.905$ of $5$} with
itself; all five ingredients survive a majority of resamples at \textbf{zero of the $35$ states};
and two disjoint halves of a state's own cohort pick top-five sets whose \textbf{median overlap is
zero}. These are winner's-curse maxima over a roughly thousand-candidate argmax on a near-flat
profile. The blend's \emph{direction} is more stable than its ingredients but not stable: split-half
cosine of the $m=5$ target is $0.987$ raw --- inflated by the common mode of \S\ref{sec:c0} --- and
$0.656$ mean-removed, IQR $[0.090,\,0.974]$. The sentence the chapter may use is therefore ``a blend
whose ingredients are unstable draws from a near-flat measured gain profile, and whose direction is
moderately stable''.

\paragraph{The $42$-state population used downstream is structurally selected.} Every number in
\S\ref{sec:c4} runs on the $42$ interview states for which a full exact gain table exists. Such a
table exists at a state precisely when the tree-grower left no truncated tail there, plus the
targeted below-floor sweeps we paid for, so \emph{missingness is deterministic in the process under
study}. The consequences are visible in the population: $27$ of the $42$ states sit under the root's
\texttt{dislike} branch, roughly a $2{:}1$ over-representation, and the cohorts nest --- $36{,}197$
user-rows drawn from $10{,}000$ users, with five states each an ancestor of twenty or more of the
others. Not truncating a population is not the same thing as it being representative of one, and we
never describe these states as ``the entire population rather than a sample''.

\section{C3: saying it in English}
\label{sec:c3}

\subsection{The variant-(a) proof of concept, and why it reports no comparison}

Before the claim-bearing renderer was built we ran the retrieval-anchored variant end to end, on
thirty blinded packets: ten real points --- five blend targets and five fine-tuned emissions --- with
ten correspondence-scrambled and ten spectrum-matched random partners. Every packet was rendered as a
question by a blinded model, judged for answerability and for whether it came from a real point by a
second blinded model that saw only question text, and re-encoded through a text-to-concept bridge with
its tag alignment asserted at all $1{,}031$ positions. All thirty rendered; all thirty re-encoded.

The pipeline works. \textbf{No comparison from it may be quoted, because the run had zero functioning
controls and an unpowered judge}, and both of those are findings about the design rather than
outcomes of it.

The scrambled arm \textbf{cannot be constructed} under this variant, which we now know structurally
and can also show. The packets scramble which point is paired with which context, but the retrieval
anchors travel with the point, so the chain from point to anchors to text stays intact for a scrambled
packet: there is no way to break the point-to-text correspondence in a pipeline whose language model
never sees the point. The measurement agrees --- the paired real-minus-scrambled re-encode difference
is median $+0.0087$, real greater at $5$ of $10$ pairs, sign test $p = 0.727$. This is the empirical
version of the disqualification argument that made the trained adapter the claim-bearing renderer in
the first place, and it is the cheapest form that argument could have taken.

The random arm was \textbf{a bug, not a control}. Its spectrum-matched draw was rescaled in the raw
space while every cosine in the analysis is read mean-removed, so the control ran at $1.84\times$ ---
up to $9.71\times$ --- the length of the point it stood in for, in the space it was compared in, while
matching the raw norm exactly, which is why nothing flagged it. The two packets that carried the
original mean-level gap are artifacts of exactly this. The draw is fixed at source and the corrected
packets are banked, but they have not been rendered or judged, so every number in this subsection
describes the uncorrected pipeline and none of them is a comparison.

The judge was \textbf{unpowered}. It was correct on $11$ of $30$ packets ($0.367$, Wilson $95\%$
$[0.219,\,0.545]$) against an all-control baseline of $0.667$, so the registered prediction that
fluency discriminates nothing is \textbf{not contradicted} and is certainly not confirmed: the
interval does not exclude $54\%$ accuracy. What is suggestive and no more is that real packets were
judged ``real'' \emph{least} often ($0.20$ against $0.60$ scrambled and $0.50$ random, all three
intervals overlapping) --- no evidence that a reader's textual heuristics track validity, and a little
that they anti-track it. A powered version needs roughly $120$ packets.

What the proof of concept buys the chapter is therefore a \textbf{harness and a design finding}: a
blinding protocol, a re-encode bridge with asserted alignment, an anchor-reference bound, and the
measured demonstration that variant (a)'s correspondence control cannot be built. Both feed the build
below, and neither is a result about rendering.

\subsection{The claim-bearing renderer, and the mechanism test}

The claim-bearing renderer is a trained adapter that maps a vector into the language model's
token-embedding space, because it is the only variant under which ``the vector conditions what is
said'' is literally true, and therefore the only one in which scrambling the vector tests
\emph{rendering} rather than \emph{retrieval}. Ours is a two-layer MLP producing eight soft prefix
tokens into a \textbf{frozen} distilgpt2, trained on the $19{,}390$ bank entries with a $20\%$
identity-held-out split stratified by channel and popularity decile; the correspondence-scrambled
twin $A'$ is trained identically on channel-preserving permuted pairs. The two arms separate by
epoch~2 and stay separated ($A'$ minus true: $+0.245$ train CE, $+0.257$ val CE; best val CE $2.991$
against $3.270$).

The registered gate ran on the item channel over $3{,}670$ held-out questions, in the tower space ---
a bridge fitted independently of the emitter and of the renderer --- with a $10$k paired bootstrap on
every cell. Criterion~1 (true minus scrambled, with the true arm reaching $\ge40\%$ of the
true-phrase ceiling) passes at $\Delta = \mathbf{+0.1114}$ $[+0.1054,\,+0.1173]$ with the true arm at
$+0.0953$ $[+0.0903,\,+0.1003]$ --- not $40\%$ but $107\%$ of the true-phrase ceiling $+0.0892$.
Criterion~3 (identifying the source vector against a same-channel decoy) passes at
$\mathbf{0.6778}$ $[0.6631,\,0.6931]$ against a ceiling-relative floor of $0.5974$ and the true
phrase's own $0.6624$. Criterion~2, an equivalence requiring $A'$ to sit within noise of a
channel-matched prior, \textbf{fails}: $A' - \text{prior} = -0.0133$ $[-0.0185,\,-0.0081]$. The
composite registered label is therefore \textbf{FAILURE and we keep it}, because a pre-registration
does not get reinterpreted after the data. What the failure says, though, is the opposite of the
threat it guarded: the twin did not smuggle signal, it sits \emph{below} knowing nothing, and it
collapsed to $61$ distinct texts over $3{,}880$ vectors against the true arm's $2{,}280$. An
equivalence control written without a direction fired on a favourable one. \textbf{The two
mechanism-bearing criteria pass with room, and the answer to the question the sheet asked --- does
the text generated from a vector depend on that vector --- is yes, at distilgpt2 scale, on held-out
questions, through an independently fitted bridge, per channel, with intervals.}

\paragraph{Machine-only synonyms, in our own space.} Exact-phrase recovery on items is
$\mathbf{0.0000}$ and token-F1 is $0.111$ --- the adapter almost never emits the gold title --- yet
its text re-encodes above the gold phrase's own ceiling. \emph{Rocky Balboa (2006)} renders as
``Rocky (2005)''; \emph{The Princess Diaries 2} as ``The Princess and the Frog (2006)'';
\emph{Zelig (1983)} as ``Theatrical Man (1962)''. These are Hewitt et al.'s machine-only
synonyms~\cite{hewitt2025neologism} appearing unprompted: text a person would grade wrong that
reproduces the vector's semantic effect better than the true surface form. Two sentences travel
together and both are ours: this is the strongest argument we have against grading rendered
questions by human plausibility, \emph{and} it is why ``above ceiling'' needs its caveat --- the
bridge prefers concept-flavoured text, and invented titles are freer to be concept-flavoured than
real ones, which is the same reason the exact-phrase floor is zero.

\paragraph{Scope of this arm.} The concept channel is not the gate but is printed: $A$ at $+0.3533$
against a required $+0.2002$, and identification $0.8476$ $[0.8000,\,0.8952]$ against a floor of
$0.7714$. The item channel is reported as an \textbf{instrument limit} downstream --- its
generic-title prior folds to $+0.245$ against the reference, a floor above the signal --- so every
number in \S\ref{sec:c4} runs on concepts. One training seed per arm; our own checklist forbids the
word ``robust'' until there are at least two, and we do not use it.

\section{C4: closing the loop, and what the translation costs}
\label{sec:c4}

This is the section the chapter exists for. At each of the $42$ states of \S\ref{sec:c1} we take the
blend target, render it through the adapter of \S\ref{sec:c3}, and push the rendered word back
through the independently fitted tower bridge to ask whether it carries the direction the point was
built from. Nothing here is an answer to a question: the reference $R_s$ is the state's own measured
ingredient direction, so the whole measurement is a property of the point and of the text. Concept
channel and blend targets only, for the two reasons already given --- the item channel's prior floor
sits above its signal, and held-out-state emissions are out of scale.

\subsection{The loop closes on the concept channel}

The registered discriminating reading is point-specificity: does a render of \emph{this} point align
with \emph{this} point's reference better than a render of some other point does. It does, at
$\mathbf{+0.28593}$ $[+0.17820,\,+0.39777]$, with the prior floor --- the same adapter with the
latent withheld --- at zero, $-0.013$ $[-0.074,\,+0.053]$. The registered faithfulness contrast
against the scrambled twin did \emph{not} separate at this $n$ (concept $+0.050$, item $-0.024$,
both n.s.), and the reason is a property of the twin rather than of the mechanism: $A'$ emits eight
distinct texts over $42$ states, so its near-constant ``horror''/``fantasy'' sometimes aligns by
luck. This is the third experiment in which $A'$'s outputs fail as a \emph{per-point} control, and we
retire it from render-level comparisons here while keeping it where it works --- training-time
separation and the held-out re-encode gate of \S\ref{sec:c3}.

\subsection{The nearest catalogue question's own text points the wrong way}

\textbf{This is the chapter's most striking measurement, and it is the motivating sentence turned
into a number.} At each state we take the bank entry nearest the emitted point in the emitter's own
head space --- cosine $0.93$, as close as the catalogue gets. Its \emph{own gold phrase}, pushed
through the same bridge, is \textbf{anti-aligned with the point's measured direction at
$\mathbf{-0.158}$ $[-0.224,\,-0.089]$}, while the rendered off-catalogue word carries that same
direction at $\mathbf{+0.204}$ $[+0.117,\,+0.291]$ --- a gap of $+0.362$ with the interval excluding
zero. \emph{The direction the policy wants to ask about is expressible, and the text of the nearest
catalogue question does not express it.}

Two caveats travel with that number wherever it appears, and we attach them here rather than in the
limitations. The $k(\varphi)$-nearest entry is itself a common-mode artefact of an anisotropic space
--- it is frequently the \emph{same} entry, \texttt{divorce} --- so ``nearest'' is doing less work
than it sounds. And the bridge favours concept-flavoured text, which is a thumb on the scale for a
one-word render against a bank phrase. The sign of the catalogue arm is large enough to survive both;
the magnitude is not to be quoted without them.

\subsection{Triangulation: where the render escapes the mode, it lands among the right neighbours}

A second, independent view asks whether the rendered word is one of the point's own nearest concepts.
Under greedy decoding the answer splits cleanly, and the split is diagnostic rather than noise. Where
the adapter emits a distinctive word ($18$ of $42$ states) the render lands at \textbf{median rank
$91$ of $1{,}031$} in the point's own ranking, with genuine bullseyes --- \texttt{action} rank
$\mathbf{1}$, \texttt{art} $4$, \texttt{indictment} $5$ (a courtroom blend: \emph{Primal Fear},
\emph{A Time to Kill}), \texttt{animals} $7$ (the Pixar branch), \texttt{military} $21$,
\texttt{adventure} $28$ (a Harry Potter blend), \texttt{science} $31$, \texttt{noir} $54$. Where it
falls back on one of three stock words (\texttt{horror}$\times9$, \texttt{religion}$\times7$,
\texttt{movies}$\times4$) the median rank is $362.5$ --- no better than the random-tag floor of
$340$. Strict top-ten membership is $4/42$ against $0/42$ for random points, with overlapping Wilson
intervals: a harsher bar that does not separate at this $n$. The reading is a
\textbf{generation-diversity limit, not a geometry failure}.

\subsection{What the translation costs, under a declared-circular answer model}

The question of how much noise translating back adds needs an answer model, and the project's ruling
of record is that a simulated geometric answerer \textbf{may} be used provided the circularity is
declared in the paper's own voice, the choice is consistent with the field's practice (Info-Synth's
geometry-dependent response model, PEBOL's simulated users), and \textbf{only a relative claim is
carried}.\todo{ruling verbatim, for provenance, not for camera-ready: ``re ndcg --- i think we have
to be circular and state it, and other papers have been too, but ideally be circular in consistent
way to others and just try and measure how translating back adds noise to the semantics of question,
something like that.''} So: two arms under the \emph{same} simulator and the same certified fold
operator, differing only in what is folded. Arm~$L$ folds the direction's own top concepts; arm~$T$
folds the concepts recovered from the rendered text. The circular component is common to both, and
the difference is the transport cost.

\textbf{Translating the direction into English and reading it back costs $\mathbf{+0.01802}$ full
$[+0.01668,\,+0.01940]$ and $\mathbf{+0.00672}$ tail $[+0.00541,\,+0.00805]$ NDCG@10}, over
$36{,}197$ user-rows across the $42$ nested states. The cost is real and non-uniform: $21$ of $42$
states have an interval above zero and \textbf{$5$ have one below it --- states where the English
beats the direction}, the best being \texttt{theatrical} at $-0.079$ $[-0.119,\,-0.040]$. It is a
genuine semantic-transport measure rather than an artefact of the two arms folding the same thing:
the overlap $|L \cap T|$ is zero at $38$ of $42$ states, mean $0.19$. Absolute rows are printed and
never headlined, under the label \emph{under a declared-circular answer model}: base
$0.16571$/$0.03041$, $L$ $0.17443$/$0.05019$, $T$ $0.15641$/$0.04347$. The summary bootstrap treats
states as strata and understates its interval; the per-state rows are the primary object.

\subsection{The cost is largely harm, not lost meaning --- and it is removable by decoding}

it is: the $18$ unique-render states pay $+0.00633$ against the $24$ repeated-render states'
$+0.02628$, a factor of $4.2$. Testing that prediction by changing \textbf{decoding only} --- same
adapter, same bridge, same reference, same simulator --- confirms the number and refutes the
mechanism we had attached to it.

direction's own top concepts: $0.17443$. Arm $T$ under greedy: $\mathbf{0.15641}$ --- \textbf{below
the no-fold baseline}. Arm $T$ under sampling: $0.16924$. \textbf{Greedy's stock words do not merely
fail to help; asking ``horror'' of a Harry-Potter-ish cohort is worse than asking nothing, and
sampling's vaguer words simply damage less.} Sampled renders are \emph{less} faithful (cosine to
$R_s$ falls from $+0.204$ to $+0.103$, interval excluding zero) and land \emph{further} from the
point's own neighbours (top-ten hits $4 \to 3 \to 1$, and the unique/repeated split inverts --- under
sampling the diverse words are mostly not neighbours and the few repeats are), and they still cost
less. The $4.2\times$ concentration on the stock-word states disappears: those states fall from
$+0.02871$ to below $+0.013$ in every non-greedy arm while the rest stay flat, which leaves the cost
roughly uniform. \textbf{So the translation cost is substantially a measure of harm from confidently
committing to the wrong concept rather than of meaning lost in translation}, and the earlier reading
``the noise is decoding diversity, not verbalisation'' survives only in the form \emph{the loss is
removable by decoding} --- what sampling removes is harm, not confusion. This is the thesis's
standing lesson that a good static choice beats a bad conditional one, arriving at the level of a
single rendered word.

\subsection{One cell retracted, and why it is reported}

The pilot also rendered the fine-tuned emissions themselves, and those cells are \textbf{retracted as
artefacts}. Thirty-four of $42$ item-hot and $32$ of $42$ concept-hot emission renders were the
\emph{empty string}, which the bridge embeds and folds anyway, producing a fixed direction that
spuriously scored $+0.276$; restricted to non-empty renders every emission effect vanishes or flips
at $n \le 9$. The cause was measured rather than guessed: the pilot emission's mean-removed norm was
$38.3\times$ the target's, so an out-of-scale input saturated the language model into immediate EOS
--- an interface mismatch between the pilot's fine-tune and the adapter's input distribution, and the
third appearance in one week of the raw-versus-mean-removed trap. Two rulings were recorded before
any replacement data existed and both bind: \textbf{an empty generation is not a rendering} ---
declared non-encodable, never folded --- and emissions are rendered only after a declared rescale to
target norm, or from a policy trained to emit in distribution. C1-final satisfies the second at
trained states (norm ratio $1.06$) and not at held-out ones ($3.48$), which is why no held-out
emission is rendered anywhere in this chapter.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Emission is licensed only where the question landscape was measured.} C1-final fits its
trained states at cosine $0.970$/$0.975$ and lands on the wrong side of zero at withheld ones
($-0.119$/$-0.011$), with the one statistic that appeared to support extrapolation matched or beaten
by the untrained warm start. The scale term does not transfer either. So the chapter's scope is not
``the policy can ask a novel question at any interview state'' but ``the policy emits, and we render,
where the gain geometry was measured'' --- $72$ emission states, and $42$ of them carried into
\S\ref{sec:c4}. Those $42$ are structurally selected, nested and dislike-heavy, and no number here is
a population estimate over interviews in general.

\paragraph{The item channel is an instrument limit, not a finding about items.} Every C4 number runs
on concepts because the item channel's own prior floor sits \emph{above} its signal --- a
generic-title prior folds to $+0.245$ against the reference --- and the tower bridge favours
concept-flavoured text. That is a property of the measuring apparatus. It means we can say nothing
about whether an off-catalogue \emph{item}-shaped direction renders faithfully, in either direction.

\paragraph{One training seed per adapter arm.} Our own checklist forbids the word ``robust'' until
there are at least two, and we do not use it anywhere in this chapter.

\paragraph{The translation cost is measured under a declared-circular answer model.} The simulated
answerer is geometric, and both arms consume it identically, which is exactly why only the difference
$L-T$ is read. No absolute NDCG row is headlined, and no version of ``asking the synthesised question
improves recommendations'' is claimed anywhere. Elsewhere in the chapter the answerer is a state
generator and nothing else: \S\ref{sec:c1}, \S\ref{sec:c3} and the faithfulness, catalogue and
triangulation readings of \S\ref{sec:c4} never simulate an answer at all.

\paragraph{No human study, and the reason is our own evidence rather than thrift.} The field's
instrument here is a plausibility panel, and \S\ref{sec:c3} measured why it would mislead us:
exact-phrase recovery of $0.0000$ and token-F1 $0.111$ on items, while the same text re-encodes above
the gold phrase's own ceiling. \emph{Rocky Balboa (2006)} rendered as ``Rocky (2005)'' is text a
rater grades wrong and the instrument grades right. A panel scoring plausibility would therefore
penalise exactly the machine-only synonyms that carry the vector's effect, and reward a fluent
confabulator that carries none, because the rater never sees the source vector. What a human study
\emph{could} decide is the question Baum and Lang actually posed --- can a real person answer this at
all --- and that remains open; if one human spend is ever made here, it should be on answerability
rather than on quality grading.

\paragraph{Inherited scope.} The scrambled-correspondence design is imported from interpretability
and from neural decoding, and only its application to a rendered elicitation question is ours; the
renderer is ELM's mechanism; and the variant-(a) proof of concept reported in \S\ref{sec:c3} had zero
functioning controls and an unpowered judge, so it contributes a harness and a design finding rather
than any comparison.
