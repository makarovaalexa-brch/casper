# Lit synthesis: does explicit dislike/negative feedback add value beyond implicit consumption?

*Token-light pass, July 2026. Not a survey — key papers + 1-line takeaways per question.*

## Q1. Is implicit feedback sufficient for top-N ranking?

- **Hu, Koren & Volinsky 2008** ("Collaborative Filtering for Implicit Feedback Datasets," ICDM, 10-year highest-impact award) — founding result: implicit-only weighted MF (confidence ∝ frequency) is enough to build a strong top-N ranker; treats all unobserved as low-confidence negative, no explicit ratings needed.
- **Rendle et al. 2009** (BPR) — formalizes the "one-class" problem: observed = positive, everything else is an unlabeled mix of true negatives and future positives; pairwise ranking on implicit-only data works well but *by construction* cannot distinguish "actively disliked" from "never seen."
- **Consensus**: implicit-only MF/BPR is the accepted default and is *sufficient* for standard top-N ranking benchmarks (ML, Netflix-style) — this is why virtually all modern top-N baselines (Mult-VAE, RecVAE, LightGCN, SASRec) are implicit-only. Sufficiency is an empirical regularity on these benchmarks, not a proof; it hasn't been stress-tested much outside them.

## Q2. Does explicit negative feedback add value beyond "everything consumed = positive"?

- **Leveraging Explicit Negative Feedback in Large-Scale Recommendation Systems: A Case Study (RecSys 2025 industry track)** — direct hit on this question at production e-commerce scale; frames explicit negatives ("not interested"/dislike) as scarce-but-clean vs. abundant-but-noisy implicit negatives, and reports gains from folding explicit negatives in as a distinct signal channel (not just more unobserved data). Could not fetch full text (403); title/abstract level confirms the framing and a positive result, not the exact effect size.
- **Beyond Positive Signals: Unlocking Implicit Negative Behaviors (2026, arXiv 2606.15252)** — argues most systems leave a lot of *implicit* negative signal (skip, short dwell, scroll-past) unused, and mining it improves sequential ranking beyond positive-only; suggests negatives don't have to be explicit to matter, but need to be captured somehow.
- **YouTube "Not Interested"/"Don't recommend channel" (industry reporting, TechCrunch 2022 + Google's own statements)** — YouTube itself says these signals carry real negative weight and improve long-run return behavior in production; but external audit found controlled-experiment efficacy (~88% suppression) far exceeds real-world efficacy (~11%), because a single explicit negative is a weak signal against weeks of positive engagement history. **Practical lesson: explicit negatives help but are low-SNR per-click and need aggregation to move ranking.**
- **Verdict**: the literature and industry practice both lean "yes, explicit negatives add value," but it's a second-order effect layered on a strong implicit-only base, not a replacement — and per-signal it's weak, gains come from volume/aggregation.

## Q3. "Consumed ≠ liked" — semantics of low ratings, and neighborhood structure of dislike

- **Correcting noisy ratings in collaborative recommender systems** (natural-noise literature) — established view: ratings are noisy mappings of a fuzzy internal opinion onto a discrete scale; a low rating conflates true dislike with miscalibration, mismatched expectation, or context.
- **How to deal with negative preferences in recommender systems: a theoretical framework** (Springer/PMC, ~2022) — explicitly says negative-preference research is thin ("has not received the same attention as positive feedback... empirical evidence is limited"); distinguishes *explicit* stated dislikes (stronger signal) from *inferred* dislikes from non-selection (weaker); does **not** develop or find a theory of dislike propagating to neighboring/similar items — treats dislikes as pointwise, not structural. This is a genuine gap, not a resolved question.
- **Takeaway**: the "consumed = liked" assumption used implicitly by top-N implicit-MF is known to be an approximation, not a truth; the field has not settled whether disliking one item tells you much about neighboring items (open/thin literature) — treat any "negative preference has neighborhood structure" claim as unverified rather than established.

## Q4. Positive-only models (Mult-VAE / RecVAE / implicit MF): is the dislike-blindness a documented ranking cost or largely irrelevant?

- **Mult-VAE (Liang et al. 2018), RecVAE (Shenbin et al. 2019)** — both purely implicit, positive-only by design; strong top-N performance is exactly why they're SOTA baselines, i.e., empirically the field treats dislike-blindness as *not* a first-order ranking cost on standard benchmarks.
- **Positive and Negative Critiquing for VAE-based Recommenders (arXiv 2204.02162)** and **Fast Multi-Step Critiquing for VAE-based Recommender Systems** — explicitly name the deficiency: "users can only express disagreement (negative critiquing) but there is no means of highlighting a desired feature," or vice versa in some designs — i.e., these VAE recommenders structurally *cannot represent* a user-stated dislike as a first-class signal, and papers had to bolt on critiquing extensions to fix it.
- **Takeaway**: the limitation is real and documented at the *architecture/interaction* level (can't take a dislike as input, can't do critiquing) even though it's largely invisible at the *static top-N ranking-benchmark* level. This maps directly onto your CASPER setting: positive-only recommenders (RecVAE/EASE) are fine as passive rankers but are the wrong substrate the moment you want to *elicit and use* a stated dislike interactively.

## Q5. Conversational/preference-elicitation: does eliciting dislikes help vs. eliciting only likes?

- **Critiquing-based CRS literature** (survey line, e.g. arXiv 2004.00646 CRS survey) — attribute-level critiquing ("I don't want this feature") is a long-standing CRS paradigm precisely because a single dislike-on-an-attribute prunes the candidate set efficiently — asking about attributes (like/dislike) "can significantly reduce the recommendation candidates," i.e., negative attribute answers carry high pruning value per turn.
- **CUPID (~April 2026)** — recent LLM-CRS work explicitly separates likes vs. dislikes extracted from dialogue and expands both into latent intents; motivated by the claim that conflating them (treating dialogue turns as undifferentiated positive signal) loses information.
- **Preference-elicitation-method studies (ScienceDirect 2024/2025)** — higher-guidance elicitation (which typically includes explicit negative/critique turns) beats low-guidance/implicit-only elicitation on match score and reduces critiquing failures.
- **Takeaway**: in the conversational/interactive setting the literature is more unanimously pro-dislike than in the static ranking setting — an explicit "no" on an attribute or item is treated as a high-value, low-noise pruning signal, distinct from the static-ranking literature where implicit-only is often sufficient.

## Bottom line (5–8 sentences)

The static top-N ranking literature is not split: implicit-only signals (Hu-Koren-Volinsky MF, BPR, Mult-VAE, RecVAE) are the accepted sufficient default, and explicit dislike is a second-order refinement, not a prerequisite — this is why positive-only models remain SOTA baselines. Explicit negative feedback does add measurable value when captured (production case studies, YouTube's own reporting), but per-signal it's weak and noisy (a single "not interested" click barely moves ranking against weeks of positive history; needs aggregation), so its marginal value is real but small in the passive-ranking regime. The literature does NOT have a settled answer on whether a low rating is a "true" negative preference vs. noise/mismatched-expectation, and even more so has not established that dislike generalizes to a neighborhood of similar items — treat that as an open/thin area, not a citable fact. Positive-only architectures' dislike-blindness is a documented structural limitation at the interaction/critiquing level (they literally cannot take a "no" as input), even though it's nearly invisible on static ranking benchmarks — meaning the cost shows up exactly when you move from passive ranking to active elicitation, which is your setting. That's also where the evidence is most one-sided in favor of dislikes: conversational/critiquing recommender literature consistently finds that eliciting an explicit dislike (on an item or attribute) is a high-value, candidate-pruning signal, more so than in static ranking. So the conditions under which dislike clearly matters are: (1) interactive/conversational elicitation rather than passive logging, (2) cold-start/sparse regimes where you can't rely on volume of positive implicit signal, and (3) attribute-level critiquing where a single "no" prunes a large candidate region. Outside those conditions — large-catalog, warm-user, implicit-abundant, static top-N — the field's consensus leans "implicit is basically sufficient, explicit negatives are a nice-to-have."

## Elicitation dislike semantics + transitivity novelty (July 2026 follow-up)

### Q1 — What does the elicitation literature mean by "dislike," and where does its value come from?

**Resolution: the elicitation literature's valuable "dislike" is overwhelmingly ATTRIBUTE/ASPECT-level, used as a soft or hard PRUNING signal — not an item-level watched-and-rated-low signal used for point-ranking.** Per-system:

- **PEBOL** (Handa et al., "Bayesian Optimization with LLM-Based Acquisition Functions for Natural Language Preference Elicitation," arXiv 2405.00981, MovieLens25M/Yelp/Recipe-MPR) — confirmed via full-text fetch. Negative feedback is attribute-level by construction: `ρt = concat("not ", at)` where `at` is a short aspect phrase (e.g. "patriotic themes"), never an item rating. Mechanism is **soft pruning via Bayesian belief update**, not hard elimination: an NLI model scores entailment of each item's description against "not patriotic," producing weights `w_it ∈ [0,1]` that update per-item Beta posteriors (`α_it = α_it-1 + w_it`, `β_it = β_it-1 + (1-w_it)`), pushing down (not zeroing) items entailing the disliked aspect. This is candidate-region downweighting, functionally close to pruning but continuous, not point-ranking of a specific item. **Data**: real item descriptions, but preferences are 100% **LLM-simulated** — GPT-3.5-turbo plays a user who "likes item i" and answers yes/no to aspect queries. No real ratings or real dislikes anywhere in the pipeline.
- **GATE** (Li & Tamkin et al., "Eliciting Human Preferences with Language Models," ICLR 2025, arXiv 2310.11589) — turned out NOT to be a movie/item recommender at all in its "content recommendation" arm; it elicits **topic-level** interest in news articles (Microsoft News Dataset, 160k articles) via free-form open-ended LM-generated questions. It does not implement an explicit dislike/pruning mechanism — preference modeling is a binary yes/no "would you read this" classifier per held-out article. **Data**: real human participants (Prolific), real article stimuli, but the "preference" being predicted is topic interest, not item-level rating; no dislike-as-pruning claim to extract here — this system is attribute/topic-level like the rest but doesn't specifically evidence a dislike-value story.
- **UNICORN / EAR / SCPR** (graph-based conversational RL, Deng et al., Lei et al.) — attribute-level. SCPR explicitly does **candidate pruning**: it uses graph-based path reasoning to prune "useless candidate attributes" from the action space once an attribute is rejected. But the literature itself flags this is a leaky heuristic: "rejecting a produced attribute does not mean the user dislikes it — maybe they like it but overlooked it, or want to try something new." So even here, attribute rejection ≈ pruning is a modeling *assumption*, not a validated fact.
- **ConTS** (Li & Lei, "Seamlessly Unifying Attributes and Items," TOIS 2021) — unifies items and attributes as arms in one Thompson Sampling framework; feedback is explicitly 4-way (like-attribute / dislike-attribute / accept-item / reject-item). This is the one system with an explicit **item-level reject** action alongside attribute dislikes — but "reject item" in ConTS means "reject an item shown as a recommendation this turn" (a simulated accept/reject on a proposed candidate), not a real historical low rating pulled from logged data. It's simulated cold-start conversation, not real ratings either.
- **Critiquing VAEs** (M&Ms-VAE, M&Ms-VAE+, "Positive and Negative Critiquing for VAE-based Recommenders" arXiv 2204.02162; "Fast Multi-Step Critiquing" arXiv 2105.00774) — explicitly **keyphrase/aspect-level**, not item-level: users critique explanation keyphrases ("less violence," "not this actor"), modeled as a third modality alongside user and item factors. The whole motivating gap named in this line ("users can only express disagreement, no means of highlighting a desired feature" or vice versa) is about attribute critique representation, never about a logged low item rating.
- **Golbandi et al. decision-tree elicitation** (RMSE-minimizing interview tree) is the one clear **item-level, point-ranking-relevant exception**: at each node users are split into "lovers/haters/unknowns" by their actual rating on a specific probe item, and the tree picks the next probe item to maximize RMSE reduction. This is real (or real-like, drawn from the ratings matrix) item-level rating data used structurally (splits the population, indirectly prunes which branch/profile-cluster a user falls into) — the closest thing to "item-level dislike doing real work" in this literature, but it's a 2010-era cold-start-onboarding system, not a modern conversational/attribute-critiquing one.
- **Rashid et al.** (2002/2008 information-theoretic rating elicitation, MovieLens) — item-level ratings, but the "value" measured is about which items to *ask about* (popularity/entropy/HELF selection strategies), not about what a low rating, once obtained, does downstream; it's an active-learning-for-elicitation-order paper, not a dislike-semantics paper.

**Does this resolve the static-vs-elicitation discrepancy?** Yes, cleanly. Static top-N literature's "explicit negatives add little" finding is about **logged item-level low ratings** feeding a point-ranking model (exactly the Golbandi/Rashid-style signal) — and that's a genuinely different, weaker signal than what the modern conversational literature is calling "dislike": a live, attribute-scoped negative that acts as a **region-pruning** operator over the candidate space in a much smaller, cold-start action space. The two literatures are not actually contradicting each other on the same object; they're measuring value of two different things (a noisy point-level rating vs. a clean, self-reported region exclusion), and — importantly — nearly all the conversational-lit "wins" for dislike are demonstrated on **LLM-simulated or synthetic aspect answers**, not on real logged dislikes. So the discrepancy is *substantially* explained by definition/dataset mismatch, exactly per your hypothesis, with Golbandi as the one item-level counterexample worth flagging in a related-work paragraph.

**Confidence note**: PEBOL and GATE claims are from full-text fetches (high confidence). UNICORN/EAR/SCPR, ConTS, and the critiquing-VAE claims are from search-snippet aggregation plus prior-session characterizations (medium confidence — mechanism descriptions are consistent across multiple independent sources but I did not pull full PDFs for ConTS/UNICORN/SCPR this pass). Golbandi and Rashid characterizations are consistent with the existing literature.

### Q2 — Is the MF-neighborhood rating-generalization/transitivity test novel?

**No direct hit for this exact test; it appears to be an open gap, not a published result** (medium-low confidence — could not verify absence exhaustively).

- The *premise* of the test — that a user's rating on item i should predict their rating on items collaboratively similar to i — is the literal founding assumption of **item-based collaborative filtering** (Sarwar et al. 2001 and the whole item-item KNN line): "a user gives similar ratings to similar items" is explicitly named as the fundamental assumption everywhere it's described. But this assumption is used operationally (as the mechanism itself) rather than being independently validated as a stand-alone diagnostic; searches did not surface a paper that isolates and reports "how well does this assumption actually hold," let alone splits it by like vs. dislike direction.
- Closest adjacent literature: the **natural-noise / rating-noise** line (ratings as noisy maps of fuzzy internal opinion) and the **"How to deal with negative preferences in recommender systems: a theoretical framework"** (Springer/PMC, ~2022) paper — already flagged in this doc's Q3 — explicitly states negative-preference research is thin and that the field has **not developed a theory of dislike propagating to neighboring/similar items**, treating dislikes as pointwise. This is a direct, citable statement that the gap you're describing is unfilled as of ~2022.
- Asymmetry-specific search turned up only tangential hits: "asymmetric response aggregation heuristics for rating prediction" (Springer, studies asymmetric *aggregation functions* for neighbor-weighted prediction, not whether like/dislike generalize differently), graph-diffusion papers on propagating negative preferences via *homophily between users* (not item-neighborhood generalization of a single user's own rating), and general negative-sampling surveys — none of these run your specific diagnostic (MF-defined item neighborhoods from binarized ratings → correlate a held-out full-scale rating against the neighbor-average rating → compare the correlation/slope for high vs. low ratings).
- No paper surfaced that frames this as a smoothness/locality diagnostic the way you're proposing (regression or correlation of target rating vs. neighbor-mean rating, symmetry-tested between the like and dislike tails).

**Verdict**: treat this as a **genuine, small, clean empirical gap** — well-motivated by the founding assumption of item-based CF (so it's not exotic), explicitly named as unresolved by the 2022 negative-preferences survey, but not run as a standalone test anywhere I could find. Worth a caveat in the paper that this is based on a search pass, not an exhaustive lit review, given the size of the CF literature — but nothing found across multiple query angles (item-based CF validation, rating locality/smoothness, positive/negative propagation asymmetry, neighbor-consistency diagnostics) contradicts novelty.

### Synthesis

- **Attribute-level dominates, and the LLM era has, if anything, sharpened this rather than reversed it.** Every system built specifically as a *question-generation policy* (OPEN, PEBOL, the 2510.12015 clarifying-questions funnel, GATE's topic classifier) asks exclusively at the attribute/aspect/feature level — never "did you like *Titanic*." The only systems where item-level content appears at all (2404.19093, CCPE) use it either as static prompt context assembled from logged ratings (not a live question) or as an artifact of fully open human-human dialogue where the human "user" happens to volunteer a title. **The trend in dedicated LLM policies (2024-2025) is toward structured attribute/feature funnels, not toward asking about items** — likely because attributes are the natural unit for LLM-generated NL questions and for Bayesian/EIG-style formalization (OPEN, PEBOL), whereas item-level asking requires a catalog-grounding step these papers mostly skip. This matches the CASPER team's own prior characterization of the pre-2026 literature (PEBOL/GATE/ConTS all attribute-first) — nothing found this pass overturns that; it is *reinforced* by three more 2024-2025 systems (OPEN, 2510.12015, and the movie user-study's live-turn behavior).
- **Negative signal is essentially always attribute-level when the assistant is the one asking; item-level dislikes only show up when they're logged/prior data, not elicited live.** OPEN has no explicit dislike ask at all (inferred from pairwise choice). PEBOL's dislike is an aspect-negation. The 2510.12015 funnel doesn't even have a dislike branch (only "No Preference" abstention). 2404.19093's item-level dislike is a *pre-existing rating* fed into the prompt as static context, never a question the assistant poses mid-conversation. CCPE (2019, human-human, the one genuinely open dialogue in the set) is the sole case where a real interviewer could and did elicit item-level dislikes live, because a human "user" can just say "I hated *X*" unprompted — but that's a property of unconstrained human dialogue, not of any LLM-driven policy design. CUPID is the interesting partial exception: it extracts already-existing item-level dislike mentions from dialogue corpora (ReDial-style, where users do sometimes name disliked titles), but it's a post-hoc extraction/representation-learning method, not a question-asking policy — it doesn't prove any system *asks* "what movie did you dislike." **Net: your hypothesis holds for every system that actively decides what to ask; item-level negatives only appear where the negative was volunteered unprompted (human dialogue) or pulled from logged history (static prior), never as a designed live probe.**
- **No one found elicits graded confidence like CASPER's {hated..loved} × {no_clue..know_well}.** Every system in this table that specifies an answer format uses binary or small-categorical responses: OPEN (pick A or B), PEBOL (yes/no per aspect, with the *belief* — not the *elicited answer* — represented continuously via Beta posteriors), 2510.12015 (yes/no/"No Preference"), 2404.19093 (no scale, only discrete rating-percentile tiers used to construct prompts, not as user-facing responses), GATE (binary classifier target). CCPE's free-text human dialogue could in principle carry graded sentiment, but it isn't captured as a structured confidence/intensity scale in the annotation scheme as described. This is a genuine, still-open gap — nothing in the 2023-2026 dialogue-elicitation literature surveyed here pairs a valence scale with an independent confidence/familiarity scale the way CASPER's two-axis {hated..loved} × {no_clue..know_well} design does.
